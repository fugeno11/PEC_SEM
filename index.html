Need for Modulation in Electronic Communications

Definition of Modulation

Modulation is the process of changing the parameters (amplitude, frequency, or phase) of a high-frequency carrier signal in accordance with the instantaneous values of a low-frequency modulating (message/intelligence) signal.

The resultant signal after modulation is called the modulated signal. The high-frequency carrier carries the message to the destination.

Fig 1.1 — Modulation block diagram

5 Reasons Why Modulation is Needed

1. Reduction in antenna height

For effective radiation, antenna height must be at least λ/4. Without modulation, transmitting a baseband signal of 15 kHz would require:

λ = c/f = (3×10⁸) / (15×10³) = 20,000 m H = λ/4 = 5000 m ← Impractical! With modulation at 1 MHz: λ = 3×10⁸ / 10⁶ = 300 m → H = 75 m ✓

2. Avoids mixing of signals

If all audio/baseband signals are transmitted at the same frequency, they mix. Modulation assigns each signal a different carrier frequency — different receivers tune to different carriers.

3. Multiplexing is possible

Modulation allows more than one signal to be transmitted over a single medium simultaneously. Each signal occupies a different frequency domain "slot".

4. Increases the range of communication

Higher-frequency signals travel longer distances with less attenuation — increasing the range of communication.

5. Improves quality of reception

High-frequency carrier signals protect information from various disturbances and noise, improving the quality of reception at the destination.

Key formula to rememberMinimum antenna height H = λ/4 = c/(4f). At 15 kHz → 5000 m. At 1 MHz (with modulation) → 75 m.

Basic Electronic Communication System

Introduction

Communication means sending, receiving, and processing of information between two or more devices. A collection of elements which work together to establish communication between a sender and receiver is called a communication system.

Fig 3.1 — Block diagram of a basic electronic communication system

Explanation of Each Block

Information Source

Originates the message — may be sound, light, picture, words.

Input Transducer

Converts non-electrical energy to electrical signal. Example: Microphone converts sound to electrical signal.

Transmitter

Transmits the electrical signal over the channel using modulation. Contains oscillators, amplifiers, modulators, and antennas.

Communication Channel

Medium through which signal travels. Types: Free space (wireless), Wire (twisted pair, co-axial), Fiber-optic cable.

Noise

Unwanted signals added to the transmitted signal. SNR = Signal Power / Noise Power. High SNR → preferred.

Receiver

Receives signal from channel, converts to original electrical signal, amplifies it. Contains demodulators, filters, amplifiers.

Output Transducer

Converts electrical signal back to non-electrical form. Example: Loudspeaker.

Destination

Final stage — where the communication system consumes the information.

TransceiverA transceiver is a device that can both transmit and receive. Examples: cellular telephones, fax machines, computer modems.

Gain, Attenuation and Decibels

1. Gain

Gain means amplification. Gain is the ratio of output to input. When output amplitude > input amplitude, the circuit has gain.

Voltage Gain (Av) = Vout / Vin Power Gain (Ap) = Pout / Pin

Cascaded stages: A_Total = A1 × A2 × A3 × A4 (product of individual gains)

2. Attenuation

Attenuation refers to a loss introduced by a circuit. If output signal is lower than input amplitude, the circuit has attenuation (gain < 1).

Attenuation A = Vout / Vin (value < 1) Total attenuation = A1 × A2 × A3

3. Decibels (dB)

Gain or loss is usually expressed in decibels (dB) — originally created to express the human ear's hearing response. The decibel is one-tenth of a Bel.

dB (voltage) = 20 log (Vout / Vin) dB (current) = 20 log (Iout / Iin) dB (power) = 10 log (Pout / Pin) Total cascaded dB = algebraic SUM of individual dB stages

Worked Examples

Problem 1 — Voltage gain in dB

Vin = 10 mV, Vout = 2 V dB = 20 log(2 / 0.01) = 20 log(200) = 46 dB

Problem 2 — Find Pout from dBm

Pout = 12.3 dBm Pout = 10^(12.3/10) × 1 mW = 17 mW
Quick rules0 dB = unity · +10 dB power = 10× · +20 dB power = 100× · +3 dB power ≈ 2×

Frequency Translation

Frequency Translation

Frequency translation is the process of transferring a signal from one frequency range to another. It is the most efficient method of transmission in a wireless communication system.

Fig 5.1 — Frequency translation block diagram

Significance

  • Converts baseband signals to passband signals for wireless long-distance transmission
  • Enables multiplexing — multiple signals on the same channel using different carrier frequencies
  • Reduces antenna height requirement
  • Allows use of Band Pass Filters (BPF) for channel selection at the receiver
S(t) × cos2π(f₁–f₂)t → BPF at f₂ → S₂(t) centered at f₂

Analog Modulation and its Types

Analog Modulation

In analog modulation, the modulating signal is an analog (continuous) signal and a high-frequency analog carrier (usually a sinusoidal wave) is used. The carrier parameters (amplitude, frequency, or phase) are continuously varied in proportion to the message signal.

A sinusoidal carrier: c(t) = Ac cos(2πfct + φ) has three parameters that can be varied — amplitude Ac, frequency fc, and phase φ. This gives three types of analog modulation:

Fig 1.1 — Types of analog modulation

1. Amplitude Modulation (AM)

The amplitude of the carrier signal is varied proportionally to the amplitude of the message signal. Frequency and phase of carrier remain constant.

s_AM(t) = [Ac + Am·m(t)] × cos(2πfct) Modulation Index: μ = Am / Ac (0 ≤ μ ≤ 1 for no distortion)

2. Frequency Modulation (FM)

The frequency of the carrier signal is varied proportionally to the amplitude of the message signal. Amplitude remains constant.

s_FM(t) = Ac cos[2πfct + 2πkf ∫m(t)dt] Modulation Index: mf = Δf / fm where Δf = frequency deviation

3. Phase Modulation (PM)

The phase of the carrier signal is varied proportionally to the message signal. Amplitude remains constant. PM and FM together are called Angle Modulation.

s_PM(t) = Ac cos[2πfct + kp·m(t)] Modulation Index: mp = kp × Am
Key comparisonAM is simple but susceptible to noise. FM has better noise immunity and audio quality (used in FM radio broadcasts). PM is used in digital communication systems.

AM Modulation and Demodulation

AM Modulation

Amplitude Modulation (AM) is the process of varying the amplitude of a high-frequency carrier signal in accordance with the instantaneous amplitude of the low-frequency modulating (message) signal, while the frequency and phase of the carrier remain constant.

s(t) = [Ac + Am·cosωmt] × cosωct = Ac·cosωct + (μAc/2)·cos(ωc+ωm)t + (μAc/2)·cos(ωc–ωm)t Where: Ac = carrier amplitude Am = message amplitude μ = Am/Ac = modulation index ωc = carrier frequency ωm = message frequency

AM Demodulation (Envelope Detector)

The most common method of AM demodulation is the envelope detector. It extracts the envelope of the AM signal, which is the original message signal.

Bandwidth of AM Signal

AM Signal contains 3 components: 1. Carrier: Ac at fc 2. Upper Sideband: (μAc/2) at fc + fm 3. Lower Sideband: (μAc/2) at fc – fm Bandwidth BW = 2 × fm (twice the highest message frequency) Power: Total Power = Pc(1 + μ²/2) Pc = Ac²/2R (carrier power)
Types of AMDSB-FC (Double Sideband Full Carrier) — standard AM. DSB-SC (Suppressed Carrier) — carrier suppressed, more efficient. SSB (Single Sideband) — only one sideband, most efficient, used in HF comm.

FM Modulation and Demodulation

FM Modulation

Frequency Modulation (FM) is the process of varying the instantaneous frequency of the carrier signal in proportion to the amplitude of the modulating signal. The amplitude of the carrier remains constant.

s_FM(t) = Ac cos[2πfct + 2πkf ∫₀ᵗ m(τ)dτ] For sinusoidal m(t) = Am cos(2πfmt): s_FM(t) = Ac cos[2πfct + mf sin(2πfmt)] Where: mf = Δf / fm = modulation index (FM) Δf = kf × Am = maximum frequency deviation fm = message signal frequency
Fig 3.1 — FM Modulator using VCO

FM Demodulation (Discriminator Method)

FM demodulation converts frequency variations back to amplitude variations. The most common method is the FM Discriminator (or Frequency Discriminator).

Fig 3.2 — FM Demodulator (Discriminator)

Bandwidth of FM (Carson's Rule)

BW = 2(Δf + fm) = 2fm(mf + 1) [Carson's Rule] Where: Δf = maximum frequency deviation fm = highest message frequency mf = Δf/fm = FM modulation index Narrowband FM (NBFM): mf < 1, BW ≈ 2fm (same as AM) Wideband FM (WBFM): mf >> 1, BW ≈ 2Δf
FM Advantages over AMBetter noise immunity (limiter removes AM-type noise). Constant transmitter power. Better audio fidelity. Used in: FM radio (88–108 MHz VHF band).

PCM Modulation and Demodulation

Pulse Code Modulation (PCM)

PCM is a digital scheme in which the amplitude of an analog signal is sampled at regular intervals, quantized, and then converted into a binary (digital) code for transmission. PCM is the most common method for converting analog information into digital form.

Three Steps of PCM

Fig 6.1 — PCM Modulator (Three steps)

Step 1 — Sampling

The analog signal is sampled at regular intervals. According to the Nyquist Theorem: sampling rate fs ≥ 2 × fm(max) to avoid aliasing.

Step 2 — Quantizing

Each sampled amplitude is rounded off to the nearest of a set of discrete levels. With n-bit encoding, we get 2ⁿ quantization levels. The error between actual and quantized value is called quantization noise.

Number of levels = 2ⁿ (n = number of bits) Quantization step = (max – min) / (2ⁿ – 1) SNR ≈ 6n dB (each added bit improves SNR by ~6 dB)

Step 3 — Encoding

Each quantized level is encoded as a binary number (n-bit code). The encoded bits are transmitted as a serial bit stream.

PCM Demodulation

Fig 6.2 — PCM Demodulator
PCM Bit RateBit Rate = fs × n bits/sample. Example: Audio CD: fs = 44.1 kHz, n = 16 bits → Bit Rate = 44.1k × 16 = 705.6 kbps per channel.

ASK Modulation and Demodulation

Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK)

ASK is a digital modulation technique where the amplitude of a sinusoidal carrier is varied to represent binary data. For binary ASK (OOK — On-Off Keying): bit '1' → carrier present (amplitude = Ac), bit '0' → carrier absent (amplitude = 0).

s_ASK(t) = A(t) × cos(2πfct) Where A(t) = Ac for bit '1', A(t) = 0 for bit '0' [OOK] For M-ary ASK: A(t) takes M different amplitude levels

FSK Modulation and Demodulation

Frequency Shift Keying (FSK)

FSK is a digital modulation technique where the frequency of the carrier is switched between two values to represent binary data. The amplitude remains constant.

s_FSK(t) = Ac cos(2πf1t) for binary '1' s_FSK(t) = Ac cos(2πf2t) for binary '0' Where f1 > f2 (or f1 ≠ f2) Frequency deviation: Δf = (f1 – f2) / 2 Bandwidth: BW = 2(Δf + fm) = f1 – f2 + 2fm

PSK Modulation and Demodulation

Phase Shift Keying (PSK)

PSK (Binary Phase Shift Keying — BPSK) is a digital modulation technique where the phase of the carrier is shifted to represent binary data. Amplitude and frequency remain constant.

s_BPSK(t) = Ac cos(2πfct + 0°) for bit '1' s_BPSK(t) = Ac cos(2πfct + 180°) for bit '0' Equivalently: bit '1' → +Ac cos(2πfct) bit '0' → −Ac cos(2πfct)

QPSK Modulation and Demodulation

Quadrature Phase Shift Keying (QPSK)

QPSK is a variant of PSK where 2 bits are encoded into each symbol by using 4 different phase shifts (45°, 135°, 225°, 315°). This doubles the bandwidth efficiency compared to BPSK.

QPSK phases: Dibit 00 → phase 45° (+45°) Dibit 01 → phase 135° (+135°) Dibit 11 → phase 225° (−135°) Dibit 10 → phase 315° (−45°) s_QPSK(t) = Ac cos(2πfct + θn) where θn ∈ {45°, 135°, 225°, 315°} Bandwidth: BWQPSK = BW_BPSK / 2 = Rs/2 (more efficient)

The three types of analog modulation techniques are:

  • AM (Amplitude Modulation): Amplitude of carrier varies with message signal. Simple but susceptible to noise. Used in: AM radio, MF band.
  • FM (Frequency Modulation): Frequency of carrier varies. Better noise immunity. Used in: FM radio (88–108 MHz), VHF band.
  • PM (Phase Modulation): Phase of carrier varies. Used in: digital communication, basis for PSK.

FM and PM together are called Angle Modulation.
