Need for Modulation in Electronic Communications

Definition of Modulation

Modulation is the process of changing the parameters (amplitude, frequency, or phase) of a high-frequency carrier signal in accordance with the instantaneous values of a low-frequency modulating (message/intelligence) signal.

The resultant signal after modulation is called the modulated signal. The high-frequency carrier carries the message to the destination.

Fig 1.1 — Modulation block diagram

5 Reasons Why Modulation is Needed

1. Reduction in antenna height

For effective radiation, antenna height must be at least λ/4. Without modulation, transmitting a baseband signal of 15 kHz would require:

λ = c/f = (3×10⁸) / (15×10³) = 20,000 m H = λ/4 = 5000 m ← Impractical! With modulation at 1 MHz: λ = 3×10⁸ / 10⁶ = 300 m → H = 75 m ✓

So modulation dramatically reduces the required antenna height.

2. Avoids mixing of signals

If all audio/baseband signals are transmitted at the same frequency (0–5 kHz), they mix with each other. A receiver cannot separate them. Modulation assigns each signal a different carrier frequency, placing each in a different "slot" of the spectrum. Different receivers tune to different carriers — no mixing.

3. Multiplexing is possible

Modulation allows more than one signal to be transmitted over a single medium simultaneously. This is possible only with modulation. Each signal occupies a different frequency domain "slot" (carrier). Without modulation, signals cannot share a single channel.

4. Increases the range of communication

Low-frequency baseband signals are heavily attenuated with distance. The modulation process increases the transmission frequency. Higher-frequency signals travel longer distances with less attenuation — increasing the range of communication.

5. Improves quality of reception

High-frequency carrier signals protect information from various disturbances. The high-frequency carrier signal protects information from various disturbances and noise, improving the quality of reception at the destination.

Key formula to remember Minimum antenna height H = λ/4 = c/(4f). At 15 kHz → 5000 m. At 1 MHz (with modulation) → 75 m.

Electromagnetic Spectrum and its Bands

Electromagnetic Spectrum

Electromagnetic waves are signals that oscillate — they have both electric and magnetic field components at a specific rate (frequency). These oscillations may occur at very low frequencies or at extremely high frequencies.

The range of electromagnetic signals encompassing all frequencies is referred to as the electromagnetic spectrum. All electrical and electronic signals that radiate into free space fall into the electromagnetic spectrum.

Basic Electronic Communication System

Introduction

Communication means sending, receiving, and processing of information between two or more devices. A collection of elements (devices) which work together to establish communication between a sender and receiver is called a communication system.

Examples: Radio broadcasting, TV broadcasting, Radio telegraphy, Mobile communication, Computer communication.

Fig 3.1 — Block diagram of a basic electronic communication system

Explanation of Each Block

Information Source

Originates the message — may be sound (human speech), light, picture, words. The message is referred to as information or intelligence signal.

Input Transducer

A device which converts one form of energy into another form (non-electrical to electrical). It converts the message signal (sound, light) into an electrical signal. Example: Microphone — a device which is placed in the information section to convert sound signals into electrical signals.

Transmitter

A device which transmits the message (electrical signal) over the communication channel. Uses a technique called modulation to send the signals. Transmitters include oscillators, amplifiers, modulators, frequency mixers, tuned circuits, and antennas.

Communication Channel

The medium through which the signal travels from transmitter to receiver. Types: Free space (wireless), Wire (twisted pair, co-axial cable), Fiber-optic cable.

Noise

Noise is the general term for all unwanted signals added to the transmitted signal along the communication channel. Noise degrades the quality of information. Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) = Signal Power / Noise Power (dB). High SNR → preferred for best performance.

Receiver

A device that receives the signal from the channel, converts it to the original electrical signal, and amplifies it. TV receiver contains amplifiers, filters, oscillators, demodulators, tuned circuits. It converts the signal sent by the transmitter back into an understandable form.

Output Transducer

Converts the electrical signal back to non-electrical form (sound or light). Example: Loudspeaker — converts electrical signal to sound.

Destination

The final stage — the place where the communication system consumes the information. Example: watching TV.

Transceiver A transceiver is a device that can both transmit and receive — it incorporates circuits that both send and receive. Examples: cellular telephones, fax machines, hand-held CB radios, computer modems.

Gain, Attenuation and Decibels

Most electronic circuits in communication are used to process signals — to manipulate signals to produce a desired result. All signal processing circuits involve either gain or attenuation.

1. Gain

Gain means amplification. If the signal is applied to a circuit such as an amplifier and the output has a greater amplitude than the input signal, the circuit has gain. Gain is the ratio of output to input.

Voltage Gain (Av) = Vout / Vin Power Gain (Ap) = Pout / Pin
Fig 4.1 — Cascaded amplifier stages

When two or more amplifier stages are cascaded, the overall gain = product of individual circuit gains: A_T = A1 × A2 × A3 × A4

2. Attenuation

Attenuation refers to a loss introduced by a circuit or component. Many electronic circuits (sometimes called "stages") reduce the amplitude of a signal rather than increase it. If the output signal is lower in amplitude than the input, the circuit has loss or attenuation.

Attenuation A = Output / Input = Vout / Vin (value < 1) Circuits with attenuation have a gain less than 1. Total attenuation = A1 × A2 × A3 (product of individual attenuations)

3. Decibels (dB)

The gain or loss of a circuit is usually expressed in decibels (dB). The decibel was originally created as a way of expressing the hearing response of the human ear to various sound levels. The decibel is one-tenth of a Bel.

dB (voltage) = 20 log (Vout / Vin) dB (current) = 20 log (Iout / Iin) dB (power) = 10 log (Pout / Pin) Total gain/attenuation = algebraic SUM of individual dB stages: A_Total (dB) = A1 – A2 + A3

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Express Pout = 12.3 dBm in watts

dBm = 10 log(Pout / 1mW) → Pout = 10^(12.3/10) × 1mW → Pout = 10^1.23 × 1mW = 17 mW = 0.017 W

Example 2 — Find power gain of 5-stage amplifier

Each stage gain = 22%, Pin = 35 mW Pout = Pin × 10^(Ap/10) Pout = 35 × 10^2.2 = 35 × 158.49 ≈ 620.95 mW Power gain = Pout / Pin = 620.95 / 35 ≈ 17.74

Frequency Translation

Frequency Translation

Frequency translation is the process of transferring a signal from one frequency range to another — moving the frequency axis from one point to another. Frequency translation occurs frequently in a wireless communication system. It is used to convert a frequency band signal to a baseband signal and is the most efficient method of transmission.

Fig 5.1 — Frequency translation block diagram

Significance of Frequency Translation

  • Converts baseband signals to passband signals for wireless long-distance transmission
  • Enables multiplexing — multiple signals on the same channel using different carrier frequencies
  • Reduces antenna height requirement (same benefit as modulation)
  • Allows use of Band Pass Filters (BPF) for channel selection at the receiver
  • Upward translation: baseband signal → centered spectrum at a new carrier f₂
  • Downward translation (demodulation): extracts the original baseband signal at receiver
S(t) × cos2π(f₁–f₂)t → BPF at f₂ → S₂(t) centered at f₂

Available Frequency Bands in Electronic Communication

Refer to Section 02 (EM Spectrum) for the full band table. Key additional notes on specific bands:

  • ELF: Includes AC power line frequencies (50 and 60 Hz) and the low end of human audio range.
  • VF: Normal range of human speech.
  • VLF: Higher end of human hearing range. Many musical instruments make sounds in this range. Used in government and military communication.
  • LF: Aeronautical and marine navigation. Also used as sub-carriers.
  • MF: AM broadcasting. Aeronautical and marine communication.
  • HF (Short Waves): All kinds of simplex broadcasting and half-duplex two-way radio comm. Broadcasts from VOA and BBC. Embassies, amateur radio (individuals become licensed "hams"), CB communication (Citizen Band — trucks, cars).
  • VHF: TV channels (2–13), FM radio broadcasting, land mobile, cellular telephones, military, radio amateurs.
  • UHF: TV channels (14–83), cellular, land mobile, military.
  • SHF (Microwave): Microwave ovens, satellite comm, telephony, computer data comm.
  • EHF: Wireless LAN, satellite communication, Radars. Specialized equipment — extremely complex and expensive.
  • Infrared: 3 types — infrared, visible, ultra-violet. Produced by light bulbs. Uses: LEDs, lasers, astronomy, TV remotes, optical fiber.

Types of Modulation Techniques
Fig 7.1 — Classification of modulation techniques

Continuous Wave (CW) Modulation

A high-frequency sine wave is used as a carrier. Three parameters can be varied:

  • AM (Amplitude Modulation): Amplitude of carrier varied with the instantaneous amplitude of the modulating signal.
  • FM (Frequency Modulation): Frequency of carrier varied with the modulating signal. Better noise immunity than AM.
  • PM (Phase Modulation): Phase of the carrier varied with the modulating signal. PM and FM together are called Angle Modulation.

Pulse Modulation

A periodic sequence of rectangular pulses is used as a carrier:

  • PAM (Pulse Amplitude Modulation): Amplitude of pulses varies in accordance with the baseband modulating signal.
  • PWM/PPM (Pulse Width/Position Modulation): Width or position of a pulse is varied.
  • PCM (Pulse Code Modulation): Digital form — analog signal is converted into digital form (coded pulse train). The result is a coded pulse train (delta modulation is further developed from PCM).
Digital modulation (Unit II) ASK, FSK, PSK, QPSK — in digital modulation, the modulating signal is in digital form. The signal is converted to digital form first (using PCM), then modulated.

Problems on Gain, Attenuation and Decibels

Formula Sheet

Voltage Gain: Av = Vout / Vin Power Gain: Ap = Pout / Pin dB (voltage) = 20 log(Vout / Vin) dB (current) = 20 log(Iout / Iin) dB (power) = 10 log(Pout / Pin) Convert dB back → ratio: Pout/Pin = 10^(dB/10) [power] Vout/Vin = 10^(dB/20) [voltage] Total cascaded dB = algebraic sum (add gains, subtract losses)

Solved Problems

Problem 1 — Voltage gain in dB

Given: Vin = 10 mV = 0.01 V, Vout = 2 V dB = 20 log(2 / 0.01) = 20 log(200) = 20 × 2.301 = 46 dB

Problem 2 — Power gain in dB

Given: Pin = 50 mW, Pout = 500 mW dB = 10 log(500 / 50) = 10 log(10) = 10 × 1 = 10 dB

Problem 3 — Find Pout from dBm

Given: Pout = 12.3 dBm Pout = 10^(12.3/10) × 1 mW = 10^1.23 × 1 mW = 17 mW

Problem 4 — Cascaded stages with a loss stage

Stages: A1 = +12 dB (gain), A2 = −6 dB (loss), A3 = +8 dB (gain) A_Total = 12 − 6 + 8 = +14 dB (net gain)

Problem 5 — Find Pout if Pin = 1 mW and gain = 20 dB

20 = 10 log(Pout / 1 mW) Pout / Pin = 10^(20/10) = 10^2 = 100 Pout = 100 × 1 mW = 100 mW
Quick rules 0 dB = unity (no change) · Positive dB = gain · Negative dB = attenuation/loss · +10 dB power = 10× power · +20 dB power = 100× power · +3 dB power ≈ 2× power

Definitions and Short Answer Questions

Modulation is the process of changing the parameters (amplitude, frequency, or phase) of a high-frequency carrier signal in accordance with the instantaneous values of the low-frequency modulating (message) signal.

The signal which contains a message to be transmitted is called the modulating signal. The high-frequency signal used as the carrier is called the carrier signal. The resultant is the modulated signal.

Modulated signal = carrier signal modified by modulating signal

Frequency translation is the process of transferring a signal from one frequency range to another — moving the frequency axis from one point to another. It is the most efficient method of transmission in a wireless communication system.

It converts a baseband (low-frequency) signal to a passband signal centered at a higher carrier frequency (upward translation), or converts a passband back to baseband at the receiver (downward translation / demodulation).

Gain means amplification. It is the ratio of output to input of a circuit. When the output amplitude is greater than the input amplitude, the circuit has gain.

Voltage Gain = Vout / Vin Power Gain = Pout / Pin dB gain = 20 log(Vout/Vin) or 10 log(Pout/Pin)

Problem: Vin = 10 mV, Vout = 2 V. Find dB gain.

dB = 20 log(2 / 0.01) = 20 × log(200) = 20 × 2.301 = 46 dB

Attenuation is a loss introduced by a circuit or component. When the output amplitude is lower than the input, the circuit has attenuation. Circuits with attenuation have gain less than 1 (negative dB).

Attenuation A = Vout / Vin (value < 1) dB = 20 log(Vout/Vin) (negative value)

Problem: Vin = 5 V, Vout = 0.5 V. Find attenuation in dB.

dB = 20 log(0.5 / 5) = 20 log(0.1) = 20 × (−1) = −20 dB

Decibel (dB) is a unit of measurement originally created to express the hearing response of the human ear to various sound levels. It is one-tenth of a Bel. Gain or loss of a circuit is usually expressed in decibels.

dB (voltage) = 20 log(Vout / Vin) dB (power) = 10 log(Pout / Pin) antilog = 10^(dB/10) = Pout/Pin

Problem: Pin = 1 mW, gain = 30 dB. Find Pout.

Pout/Pin = 10^(30/10) = 10^3 = 1000 Pout = 1000 × 1 mW = 1000 mW = 1 W
